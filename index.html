Everything below comes from the Official Rules of the 2027 CFA Institute Research Challenge (22 pages, "2026-2027 season") and the CFA Society Singapore kick-off slides of 8 Sep 2026. Page numbers are given so anyone can check.

What we are writing

An equity research report: the kind of document a bank analyst writes to tell investors whether to buy, hold or sell a share, and at what price it should trade in 12 months (the target price). We write it "from the perspective of an independent research analyst", using public information only (Rules p. 9, 16).

Format of the written report

  • At most 10 A4 pages, plus an appendix of at most 10 A4 pages. The official CFA front cover does not count, and we may only fill in its highlighted fields (Rules p. 16).
  • The first page must show: company name, exchange, ticker, sector, recommendation (buy/hold/sell), current price with its date, and target price with the % up or down (Rules p. 16).
  • Seven sections are required: Business description; Industry overview and competitive positioning; Investment summary; Valuation; Financial analysis; Investment risks; Environmental, social and governance (Rules p. 16).
  • Every fact, chart and table is cited in one system. The team uses Harvard style: (Keppel Ltd, 2026, p. 12) in the text and a reference list at the end (Rules p. 4, 16).

  • Faculty advisor (Prof. Rick Marchese): at most 10 hours before the report is submitted. Industry mentor (Samir Mehta, CFA): at most 6 hours. Going over means disqualification of the team (Rules p. 10-11). Every meeting goes in the help-hours log.
  • They can guide and give feedback, but cannot write content or do analysis for us (Rules p. 10).
  • No other finance professional may help, including mock judging panels (Rules p. 7, 9, 11).
  • Broker reports (UBS, Jefferies, DBS...) can be read, but anything we use from them must be cited as a broker view. Copying them is plagiarism and means disqualification (Rules p. 4-5, 8).
  • Each member must disclose any shares held in Keppel (Rules p. 4, cover p. 18).

Artificial intelligence

AI tools are allowed "in a responsible, transparent, and ethically sound manner", but material use must be disclosed and documented, and presenting AI-generated content as wholly original human work is prohibited (Rules p. 8). Judges are likely to ask about it (Rules p. 17), so each of us should be able to say: which tool, for what, how we checked the output, and what we decided ourselves. (a) How to do this part, step by step (for a total beginner)

Step 0. What the judges want from this part

The Business Description and the Industry Overview are the "story" pages. A reader who has never heard of Keppel must finish them knowing: what Keppel sells, who pays it, how it makes money, what is changing, who it competes with, and why that matters for the share price. Every sentence should prepare a valuation choice made later (SOTP, comps, DCF). If a paragraph does not help the valuation or the risks, cut it.

Step 1. Understand the business model in one picture

Keppel calls itself a "global asset manager and operator" (Keppel Ltd, 2026c, p. 1). In plain words:

  1. Keppel raises money from big investors (pension funds, insurers, sovereign funds). These investors are called Limited Partners (LPs): they give the money and have limited say in daily decisions.
  2. Keppel acts as the General Partner (GP): it chooses, buys, builds and runs the assets (power plants, data centres, subsea cables, offices).
  3. Keppel earns money three ways, which it now reports separately ("horizontal reporting", Keppel Ltd, 2026b, p. 19): - Asset management: fees for managing other people's money. 1H 2026 net profit S$72m. - Sponsor stakes and co-investments (SSCI): Keppel's own money invested next to the LPs, so it earns like an investor. 1H 2026 net profit S$175m. - Operating platform: Keppel runs the assets itself (selling electricity, cooling, leasing, technology solutions). 1H 2026 net profit S$349m.

An analogy: think of a restaurant group that (1) manages restaurants owned by others for a fee, (2) owns a small share in each of those restaurants, and (3) also runs the kitchens. Three income streams from the same restaurants.

Step 2. Learn the five words you will use all the time

Term Plain meaning Keppel's own definition or figure
FUM (Funds under Management) The total size of the assets Keppel manages for itself and for clients. Bigger FUM usually means more fees. Gross asset value of investments plus uninvested commitments on a leveraged basis (Keppel Ltd, 2026c, p. 15). S$95bn at end-2025 (Keppel Ltd, 2026a, p. 5), S$106bn at end-July 2026 (Keppel Ltd, 2026b, p. 8).
Fee-related earnings / asset management fees Fees charged for managing the money: management fees (a % of capital each year), acquisition and divestment fees, and performance fees. Asset management fees S$453m in FY2025 (Keppel Ltd, 2026a, p. 5), S$354m in 9M 2026 (Keppel Ltd, 2026c, p. 6).
Carried interest ("carry") A performance fee: the GP keeps a share of the profit (typically 20%) once the fund beats a minimum return called the hurdle. Large but irregular. "typically 20% of the investment returns, upon the fund achieving a return above the benchmark or hurdle IRR" (Keppel Ltd, 2026f, p. 13).
Recurring income Profit that comes back every year (fees, rent, electricity sales), as opposed to one-off gains from selling or revaluing assets. Investors pay a higher multiple for it. Net profit in the "Operational" row (Keppel Ltd, 2026f, p. 15). S$941m in FY2025, up 21% (Keppel Ltd, 2026a, p. 9); S$467m in 1H 2026, up 13% (Keppel Ltd, 2026b, p. 5).
Fee-to-FUM ratio Fees divided by FUM: how much Keppel earns per dollar managed. Keppel assumes about 50 basis points (0.50%) to reach "potential asset management fees" of S$1bn on S$200bn FUM (Keppel Ltd, 2026f, p. 11). Check: 0.50% x 200bn = 1.0bn.

Two more words specific to Keppel. "The New Keppel" = the business it keeps, excluding the "Non-Core Portfolio for Divestment" (old rigs, residential land, some properties, M1 telco) that it is selling (Keppel Ltd, 2026c, p. 15). Always say which perimeter a number refers to: 1H 2026 New Keppel net profit was S$530m but group net profit was only S$155m (Keppel Ltd, 2026b, p. 16).

Step 3. Describe a conglomerate's segments without getting lost

Keppel reports two ways at once. Write one short paragraph per view and one table.

  • Vertical view, by segment: Infrastructure (power, gas, district cooling, waste-to-energy, decarbonisation solutions), Real Estate (sustainable urban development, property funds, Keppel REIT), Connectivity (data centres, subsea cables, technology solutions, M1 telco now non-core).
  • Horizontal view, by type of income: asset management, SSCI, operating platform (Step 1).

Rule for the report: give each segment its share of profit with the period, and say what drives it (Infrastructure = power spreads and contracts; Real Estate = property values and fund fees; Connectivity = data-centre leasing and cable contracts).

Step 4. Tell the transformation story as a timeline, not as an essay

Keppel was an offshore rig builder and conglomerate. Use 5 to 6 dated steps (see draft text): Vision 2030 announced; O&M business combined with Sembcorp Marine into Seatrium (2023); asset monetisation programme since Oct 2020; bifurcation into New Keppel versus Non-Core (2025); FUM S$100bn reached (July 2026); target S$200bn FUM by end-2030. Each step must carry a date and a source.

Step 5. Industry overview: four tailwinds, each with one hard number

Do not write "the industry is growing". For each driver, give one sourced figure and say what it means for Keppel's fees or operating income:

  1. Private markets and real assets: LPs keep shifting money to private infrastructure and real assets; fundraising is concentrated in large managers.
  2. Data centres and AI: power-hungry data centres need power, cooling and land; Asia is short of power.
  3. Energy transition: new low-carbon power, cooling and waste-to-energy contracts with 10 to 15 year visibility.
  4. Singapore and Asian real estate: the slowest-growing piece; Keppel is selling much of it.

Step 6. Porter five forces for an asset manager-operator

Porter's five forces measure how much profit an industry can keep. For an asset manager the "buyers" are the LPs and the "suppliers" are the people (investment teams) and the deal sellers.

Force Question to ask Keppel reading
Rivalry How many managers chase the same LP money and the same deals? High: global giants (Brookfield, Blackstone, Macquarie, KKR) and regional players (CapitaLand Investment, Mapletree) bid for the same assets.
Bargaining power of buyers (LPs) Can LPs push fees down? Medium-high: big LPs negotiate fee discounts and co-investment with no fees. Keppel's fee-to-FUM is assumed at about 0.50% (Keppel Ltd, 2026f, p. 11).
Bargaining power of suppliers Talent, asset sellers, equipment (turbines, chips, cables), banks Medium: senior dealmakers are scarce; gas supply for power plants is a real supplier risk (Keppel Ltd, 2026e, p. 3).
Threat of new entrants Can a newcomer raise a S$1bn infrastructure fund? Low-medium: track record, scale and operating skills are hard to copy; new money tends to go to established managers.
Threat of substitutes Can LPs get the same exposure elsewhere? Medium: listed REITs and infrastructure trusts, direct investing by sovereign funds, cheaper index products.

Write the conclusion in one sentence: the industry rewards scale and operating skill, which is why Keppel's edge is that it both manages and operates.

Step 7. Build the peer table (feeds SOTP and comps for Part D)

Pick 6 to 9 listed companies. For each: ticker, country, what it does, which part of Keppel it is comparable to. Use pure asset managers for the asset management slice of a SOTP, power and utility companies for the operating platform, and REITs or property developers for real estate. Do not compare a whole conglomerate to one pure player without saying so.

Step 8. Check before handing over

Every figure has a reference code and a page; every date is from the document body; no forecast is presented as a fact; management quotes are word for word with quotation marks.

Business description

Keppel Ltd is a Singapore-based "global asset manager and operator" in Infrastructure, Real Estate and Connectivity (Keppel Ltd, 2026c, p. 1). It raises capital from institutional investors into private funds and listed vehicles, invests its own capital alongside them, and operates many of the underlying assets, which the CEO describes as "two streams of income": "asset management fees and operating income from these assets" (Straits Times, 2026). Since 1H 2026 the company reports profit by income type: in 1H 2026 the New Keppel, which excludes the Non-Core Portfolio for Divestment, earned S$530m, of which S$72m from asset management, S$175m from sponsor stakes and co-investments (SSCI) and S$349m from the operating platform, less S$66m of corporate costs (Keppel Ltd, 2026b, p. 19). By segment, Infrastructure contributed S$538m, Connectivity S$77m and Real Estate a loss of S$19m after a S$51m loss on the dividend in specie of Keppel REIT units (Keppel Ltd, 2026b, p. 20).

FUM reached S$95bn at end-2025 (Keppel Ltd, 2026a, p. 5) and S$106bn at end-July 2026, passing the S$100bn end-2026 target ahead of schedule (Keppel Ltd, 2026b, p. 8). Asset management fees were S$453m in FY2025 (Keppel Ltd, 2026a, p. 5) and S$354m in 9M 2026, up 18% year on year, with Real Estate still the largest fee pool (S$195m) ahead of Infrastructure (S$107m) and Connectivity (S$52m) (Keppel Ltd, 2026c, p. 6). Keppel added S$19.5bn of new FUM in the year to date, about half from Infrastructure and Connectivity strategies (Keppel Ltd, 2026g, pp. 2-3).

The next target is S$200bn of FUM by end-2030, which management links to "potential asset management fees" of about S$1bn assuming a fee-to-FUM ratio of about 50 basis points, and to more than 70% of earnings "expected from infrastructure solutions supporting AI" (Keppel Ltd, 2026f, p. 11). The Annual Report lists "Achieve Vision 2030 financial targets" among management's priorities (Keppel Ltd, 2026a, p. 14), and under the same 2030 horizon the S$13.7bn Non-Core Portfolio is "targeted to be substantially monetised" (Keppel Ltd, 2026f, p. 11). The CEO restated that "the goal for Keppel is to monetise all the non-core before 2030" (Keppel Ltd, 2026e, p. 5).

The transformation is recent. Keppel divested its offshore and marine business in 2023 (S$4.7bn, including Sembcorp Marine, now Seatrium, shares that were distributed or held in a segregated account) (Keppel Ltd, 2026a, p. 9), and its outgoing Chairman wrote that the company "exited its offshore and marine business, shed its conglomerate structure and repositioned itself as an asset-light global asset manager and operator" (Keppel Ltd, 2026a, p. 8). Since October 2020 it has announced S$16.9bn of asset monetisation, including S$3.7bn in 2026 to date, above the S$2-3bn full-year target (Keppel Ltd, 2026c, p. 13). The largest pending items are six legacy rigs sold to the Keppel Offshore Fund (S$1.2bn), with Apollo as LP, and 51% of Keppel South Central sold to Keppel REIT (S$1.6bn) (Keppel Ltd, 2026c, pp. 5, 13). The sale of M1's telco business was not completed (Keppel Ltd, 2026c, p. 13). This non-core drag explains why 9M 2026 group profit fell 40% while New Keppel profit rose more than 20% (Keppel Ltd, 2026c, p. 3).

Temasek Holdings is the anchor shareholder with a 21.46% total interest, and BlackRock holds 5.42%, as at 2 March 2026 (Keppel Ltd, 2026a, p. 234). Industry overview and competitive positioning

Keppel sits where three structural trends meet. First, institutional investors keep shifting capital to private infrastructure. Global infrastructure fundraising reached a record of nearly US$200bn in 2025, up about 58% on 2024, while fundraising across other private-market asset classes declined; the growth came from North America and Europe, and Asia-Pacific fundraising fell (McKinsey & Company, 2026). The market has since cooled, with US$40.8bn raised in 1H 2026 (Macfarlanes, 2026), and capital concentrates in very large funds. This favours managers with scale and a record: Keppel added S$19.5bn of new FUM in 2026 to date and reports a deal pipeline of about S$23bn (Keppel Ltd, 2026g, pp. 2-3), but it remains small next to Brookfield Asset Management, whose fee-bearing capital alone is US$682bn (Brookfield Asset Management, 2026). Second, AI and cloud demand require power, cooling and connectivity: in Asia-Pacific the data-centre development pipeline reached a record 26.5GW in 1H 2026 while vacancy still fell from 10.9% to 10.3%, and power availability has become the main bottleneck (Cushman & Wakefield, 2026). Singapore rations new capacity: its second call for applications allocated 200MW shared equally between Digital Realty, Equinix, Keppel Data Centres and ST Telemedia (King & Wood Mallesons, 2026), which matches the 50MW Keppel reports. Keppel's portfolio spans 39 data centres with about 900MW of gross power capacity (Keppel Ltd, 2026c, p. 12). Third, the energy transition gives long-dated contracts: Keppel's decarbonisation business holds S$8.1bn of contracted revenue with 10-15 year visibility, and the 600MW Keppel Sakra Cogen Plant raised its power capacity to 1,900MW (Keppel Ltd, 2026c, p. 7; 2026b, p. 10). Real estate is the weakest leg, with over S$2.4bn of property monetisation announced this year (Keppel Ltd, 2026c, p. 11).

Competition comes from global alternative managers (Brookfield, Macquarie Asset Management, Blackstone, KKR) that have far larger FUM, and from regional players: CapitaLand Investment (S$128bn of FUM at 30 September 2026, CapitaLand Investment, 2026) and Mapletree in real estate, and Sembcorp Industries in Singapore power and renewables. ESR, a former listed peer in Asian logistics real estate, was taken private in 2025 [TO VERIFY]. Keppel's differentiation is its in-house engineering and operating capability, which management says lets it originate proprietary deals, with an expected IRR to its private funds and co-investors of about 30% on the Bifrost cable and about 15% on Sakra (Keppel Ltd, 2026f, p. 4). A five forces reading points to high rivalry and strong LP bargaining power, offset by high barriers to entry for operator-managers with track records; the industry rewards scale, which makes the pace of FUM growth the key variable for the valuation.

(a) How to do this part, step by step (for a total beginner)

Step 1. Understand what kind of company Keppel is now

Until 2022 Keppel was a conglomerate: it built oil rigs (Offshore & Marine, "O&M"), developed property, ran a telco (M1) and owned power plants. Since then it has turned itself into a global asset manager and operator. Think of a landlord who used to own every building himself and now mostly manages buildings owned by pension funds, keeping a small stake and charging a management fee.

Keppel now earns money in four ways:

  1. Asset management fees: fees charged to investors (called Limited Partners, LPs: pension funds, sovereign wealth funds) for managing their money in Keppel's private funds and listed REITs/trusts. Very stable.
  2. Operating income: profit from running assets (power plants, district cooling, data-centre services, subsea cables).
  3. Sponsor stakes and co-investments (SSCI): Keppel's own money invested next to its LPs.
  4. Valuation and capital recycling gains: revaluation of properties and profits when assets are sold. Lumpy, one-off by nature.

Why it matters: the market pays a high multiple for fees and recurring operating income, and a low multiple for one-off gains. Your job in Part B is to show how much of the profit is of each kind, and whether the mix is improving.

Step 2. Learn Keppel's own profit definitions before you compare years

Step 3. Net profit by segment

Keppel reports profit by business (Infrastructure, Real Estate, Connectivity, Corporate) and also "horizontally" by type of income (asset management, operating, valuation, capital recycling). Use both.

Worked example, FY2025 (Keppel Ltd, 2026j, p. 21): Infrastructure 803 (73% of New Keppel profit), Real Estate 273 (25%), Connectivity 175 (16%), Corporate activities (151). The percentages add to more than 100% because Corporate is negative (head-office costs and interest not allocated to segments).

What to write: Infrastructure (mainly the Singapore power business) is the profit engine, so the earnings depend on Singapore electricity spreads, not only on fund management.

Step 4. Recurring versus one-off income

Recurring income = asset management + operating income (Keppel's definition, horizontal reporting). Compute its share of core profit yourself:

FY2025: 941 / 1,100 = 85.5%
1H2026: 467 / 530 = 88.1%

Sources: (Keppel Ltd, 2026i, p. 2; 2026h, p. 1). Careful: in FY2024 restated, recurring income (779) was 98% of New Keppel profit (793), so the share fell in FY2025 even though recurring income grew 21%. Why? Because valuation and divestment gains grew faster (206 + 104 in FY2025 against 146 + 9 in FY2024; Keppel Ltd, 2026j, p. 20). A falling share is not bad news by itself, but it means more of the profit is lumpy.

Step 5. Funds under management (FUM) and fees

FUM is the value of assets Keppel manages for others (including uninvested commitments, on a leveraged basis: Keppel counts the debt-funded part too). Fees are what it charges on them.

Worked example: fee-to-FUM in FY2025 = fees / average FUM = 453 / ((88 + 95) / 2 × 1,000) = 49.5 basis points (1 basis point = 0.01%). Keppel reports 48 bps (Keppel Ltd, 2026j, p. 27); the small gap comes from its own averaging. FUM grew from S$37bn (2020) to S$95bn (2025), a 20.8% compound annual growth rate (CAGR), and fees from 180 to 453 (20.3% CAGR), both in line with the "21%" and "20%" Keppel shows (Keppel Ltd, 2026j, p. 9).

Trap: fees are counted at 100% for platforms Keppel controls, even if Keppel owns only part of them, and include fees on Keppel's own sponsor stakes. So fees are not the same as fee revenue in the profit and loss account (asset management revenue was 112 in 1H2026 against 200 of "fees"; Keppel Ltd, 2026h, p. 30 and 4). Use asset management net profit (189 in FY2025) to judge profitability.

Step 6. Balance sheet and gearing

Net gearing = net debt / total equity. Net debt = borrowings + lease liabilities − cash.

FY2025: 9,127 / 11,186 = 0.82x

(Keppel Ltd, 2026i, p. 12). For an asset manager, a better yardstick is net debt to EBITDA (EBITDA = profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation; Keppel includes the share of associates). Our recomputation for the whole group in FY2025: 1,317 + 421 − 117 + 170 = 1,791, so 9,127 / 1,791 = 5.1x, the same as Keppel's group figure (Keppel Ltd, 2026j, p. 18). For the New Keppel alone Keppel reports 2.0x (FY2025) and 1.4x (June 2026): most of the debt sits in the Non-Core Portfolio, especially the legacy rigs.

Step 7. Return on equity (ROE)

ROE = net profit attributable / average shareholders' funds (average of opening and closing). Always recompute:

FY2025: 788.5 / ((10,754 + 10,463) / 2) = 7.4%

Same as reported (Keppel Ltd, 2026i, p. 57). The "New Keppel ROE" of 18.7% divides core profit by only the equity Keppel allocates to the New Keppel; we cannot recompute it because that equity split is not disclosed. Say so in the report.

Step 8. Cash flow, asset monetisation and the legacy O&M assets

Keppel defines free cash flow (FCF) as operating plus investing cash flow. FY2025: 661.5 − 286.0 = 375.5; Keppel reports 611 because it adds a 235 financing item linked to Global Marine Group (Keppel Ltd, 2026i, p. 2, 17-18). Always show both.

Asset monetisation = selling assets to recycle capital. Keppel counts "announced" deals (gross value plus debt that leaves the balance sheet), which is not cash received. Since October 2020 it has announced S$16.9bn, including S$4.7bn for the O&M combination with Sembcorp Marine in 2023 (Keppel Ltd, 2026c, p. 13). "Completed and realised" cash is much smaller: about S$940m in 9M2026 (Keppel Ltd, 2026g, p. 1).

Legacy O&M assets = what remains of the rig-building business: Seatrium shares, the legacy rigs held by Rigco, Floatel, KrisEnergy and Dyna-Mac. They lost 156 in FY2025 and drove most of the 375 Non-Core loss in 1H2026 through rig impairments (Keppel Ltd, 2026j, p. 25; 2026b, p. 17).

Common mistakes to avoid

  1. Mixing an "as first reported" year with a "restated" year in the same column (FY2024 revenue is 6,601 as reported and 5,784 restated).
  2. Calling FY2023 a record year without saying that ~3,300 of the 4,067 came from the one-off O&M disposal gain (Keppel Ltd, 2024a, p. 1).
  3. Taking "New Keppel ROE 18.7%" as the shareholder return; the statutory ROE was 7.4%.
  4. Treating announced monetisation as cash.
  5. Adding the in-specie Keppel REIT units to the dividend without saying they are valued at a market price on a given day (0.98 on 3 Feb 2026).
  6. Using Quartr AI or broker numbers as sources: they are leads only.

Historical financial analysis

From conglomerate to asset manager. Keppel has spent five years dismantling its conglomerate structure. The 2023 combination of Keppel Offshore & Marine with Sembcorp Marine generated a disposal gain of about S$3.3bn and lifted FY2023 net profit to S$4.07bn, but the shares received were largely distributed to shareholders in specie, worth about S$2.19 per Keppel share (Keppel Ltd, 2024a, p. 1, 51). Since FY2025 the group reports a "New Keppel", which excludes a S$13.5bn (end-2025) Non-Core Portfolio of legacy rigs, residential landbank and other assets earmarked for sale by 2030 (Keppel Ltd, 2026i, p. 3, 59). Funds under management rose from S$55bn in 2023 to S$95bn at end-2025 and S$106bn in July 2026, helped by the 2024 acquisition of Aermont Capital (Keppel Ltd, 2025a, p. 2; 2026h, p. 1).

Earnings mix and quality. New Keppel net profit rose 39% to S$1.10bn in FY2025 and a further 25% to S$530m in 1H2026, against restated bases of S$793m and S$424m (Keppel Ltd, 2026i, p. 2; 2026h, p. 47). Infrastructure, largely the Singapore integrated power business, contributed 73% of FY2025 core profit, so earnings remain exposed to power spreads (Keppel Ltd, 2026j, p. 21). Recurring income reached S$941m, or 85.5% of core profit on our calculation, down from 98% in restated FY2024 because valuation and divestment gains rose to S$310m (Keppel Ltd, 2026j, p. 20). In 1H2026 a further S$127m came from valuation and divestment gains on sponsor stakes (Keppel Ltd, 2026b, p. 19). Statutory profit tells a weaker story: net profit attributable fell from S$940m in FY2024 to S$789m in FY2025 and to S$155m in 1H2026, as rig impairments and the reversal of the M1 Telco disposal hit the Non-Core Portfolio (Keppel Ltd, 2026i, p. 8; 2026h, p. 2).

Returns. Reported ROE was 7.4% in FY2025 and an annualised 3.6% in 1H2026; we recompute 7.4% and 3.0% respectively (Keppel Ltd, 2026i, p. 57; 2026h, p. 47). Keppel's New Keppel ROE of 18.7% (FY2025) and 15.0% (1H2026) rests on an internal equity allocation that is not disclosed, so we treat it as a target metric rather than a measured return.

Balance sheet and gearing. Net debt fell from S$9.87bn at end-2023 to S$9.13bn at end-2025 (net gearing 0.90x to 0.82x) before edging up to S$9.33bn (0.84x) in June 2026 after dividends and about S$240m of buybacks in 1H2026 (S$356m since July 2025, of which S$116m in 2H2025) (Keppel Ltd, 2025a, p. 11; 2026i, p. 12; 2026h, p. 3, 12). Group net debt to EBITDA of 5.1x (our recomputation: 5.09x) is high for an asset manager, but the New Keppel ratio is 1.4x, confirming that leverage sits mostly in assets being sold (Keppel Ltd, 2026j, p. 18; 2026b, p. 6).

Cash and asset monetisation. Operating cash flow has been weak relative to profit: S$58m in FY2023, S$200m in FY2024 and S$662m in FY2025 (Keppel Ltd, 2025a, p. 16; 2026i, p. 17). Keppel has announced S$16.9bn of monetisation since October 2020, including S$3.7bn in 2026 up to 29 October, but completed and realised only about S$940m (cash received plus debt removed from the balance sheet) (Keppel Ltd, 2026c, p. 13; 2026g, p. 1). The gap between announced and realised value, and the failed M1 sale, are the main execution risks.

Dividends. The ordinary dividend has been stable at 33-34 cents since FY2021. For FY2025 Keppel added a special dividend of about 13 cents (2 cents cash plus Keppel REIT units), sized at 15% of the S$1.6bn of monetisation completed, taking the total to about 47 cents (Keppel Ltd, 2026i, p. 4). The 34-cent ordinary dividend equals 56% of core profit but 78% of statutory profit on our calculation.

Environmental, social and governance

Energy transition. Keppel cut its own Scope 1 and 2 emissions by 87.6% from 2020 to 21,312 tCO2e in 2025, partly through renewable energy certificates (Keppel Ltd, 2026k, p. 45). The material figure is Scope 3: 6.7m tCO2e, 77% from the use of sold products, mainly natural gas, and up 11% year on year (Keppel Ltd, 2026k, p. 45). The power business is still gas-fired, although the new 600 MW Sakra plant, operating since May 2026, is hydrogen-compatible, and the renewables portfolio reached 4.7 GW against a 7 GW target for 2030 (Keppel Ltd, 2026h, p. 1; 2026k, p. 10). Keppel has raised about S$5bn of sustainability-linked financing since 2024 (Keppel Ltd, 2026k, p. 58).

Governance. Seven of eight directors are independent, women hold 25% of board seats (target 30% by 2030) and Keppel ranks second of 467 in the Singapore Governance and Transparency Index (Keppel Ltd, 2026k, p. 10, 57). Temasek has a total interest of 21.46% (20.64% direct), and interested person transactions with Temasek-linked entities, including a S$131m divestment to Keppel Infrastructure Trust, need monitoring (Keppel Ltd, 2026a, p. 219, 234). CEO total remuneration was S$9.5m in FY2025, excluding carried interest (Keppel Ltd, 2026a, p. 73). The Brazil Operation Car Wash case still lingers through a S$68.4m indemnity claim against Seatrium now in arbitration (Keppel Ltd, 2026a, p. 170).

ESG view. Strong governance and clear disclosure, with emissions risk concentrated in Scope 3 gas sales rather than in Keppel's own operations.

What the judges want

Financial analysis is worth 20 points and is shared between the past (Part B) and the forecast (this part). Investment risks (15 points, Rules p. 19) are covered further down this page. For the forecast, judges look for a model driven by a few clear assumptions that link back to the business story, compared with management targets and with what other analysts expect, and for honest scenarios rather than one single "right" number.

How to build the forecast for Keppel, step by step

Step 1: forecast profit by segment, not revenue alone

Keppel's revenue mixes very different businesses, so the useful forecast is net profit by segment (Infrastructure, Real Estate, Connectivity, plus corporate costs), and inside it the asset management fees. For each part ask what drives it:

  • asset management fees follow funds under management (FUM) and the fee rate;
  • infrastructure profit follows power prices, contracted capacity and new projects;
  • real estate profit follows property sales and rents;
  • connectivity follows data-centre capacity and occupancy;
  • one-off gains come from asset sales (the asset monetisation programme) and should be kept separate.

Write each assumption as one sentence with a source ("Keppel had S$... of FUM at 30 Jun 2026 (Keppel Ltd, 2026b, p. x)").

Step 2: separate recurring income from one-offs

Judges reward teams that show how much of the profit comes back every year. Forecast recurring income and one-off gains on separate lines.

Step 3: check against three anchors

  1. Management targets: Vision 2030, FUM targets, asset monetisation targets, dividend policy.
  2. Consensus: what analysts expect on average (label it as a broker view).
  3. History: the segment profits of the last five years (Part B).

If our numbers are far from management or consensus, explain why in one sentence. That gap is often the heart of the investment thesis.

Step 4: balance sheet and cash

Forecast net debt from operating cash flow plus asset sale proceeds, minus investments and dividends. Net gearing (net debt / equity) should stay in the range management has given.

Step 5: three scenarios

Bull, base and bear, each with one coherent story (for example bear = fundraising slows and asset sales are delayed). Give each a rough probability. The valuation runs on each scenario.

Step 6: hand the numbers to Part D

One table per year, 2026 to 2028: FUM, fee income, segment net profit, recurring income, one-offs, net debt, dividends per share. Part D reads them and does not change them.

What the judges want

Valuation is worth 20 of the 100 report points (Rules p. 19). CFA Institute's guidance says a strong valuation uses more than one method, explains every key input, and ends with a target price that follows from the numbers (CFA Institute, 2020, p. 3). For a group like Keppel, judges expect a sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) valuation, because one single multiple cannot capture a business that mixes fee income, operating assets, property and listed stakes. They check three things: is each part valued with a method that fits it, are the inputs sourced, and is the gap to the share price explained (the "conglomerate discount").

How to do this part, step by step

Step 1: fix the price and the share count

Take the closing price on one date and use it everywhere (for the preliminary report: Friday 13 Nov 2026). Shares outstanding come from the latest company filing, net of treasury shares. Price × shares = market capitalisation.

Step 2: list the parts

Write down every block of value Keppel owns, in the way the company reports it. Typically:

  1. the asset management business (fees earned on funds under management);
  2. stakes in listed REITs and trusts it sponsors (Keppel DC REIT, Keppel REIT, Keppel Infrastructure Trust...);
  3. operating infrastructure businesses (power, renewables, environmental infrastructure);
  4. real estate it owns directly (land bank, development projects);
  5. connectivity (data centres, subsea cables, and any telecom stake);
  6. non-core and legacy assets (for example the legacy offshore and marine assets);
  7. net debt, perpetual securities and minority interests, which are subtracted at the end.

Step 3: value each part with the method that fits

Part Usual method Why
Asset management Multiple of fee-related earnings (P/E of listed asset managers) Fees are recurring; peers such as Brookfield or CapitaLand Investment give the multiple
Listed REIT and trust stakes Market value: number of units × unit price on the same date The market already prices them
Operating infrastructure DCF or EV/EBITDA of utility and infrastructure peers Long-lived, cash-generating assets
Real estate held directly Book value or revalued value (RNAV), possibly with a discount Property values are disclosed in the accounts
Connectivity, data centres EV/EBITDA of data-centre peers, or book value Growth assets; few earnings yet
Non-core and legacy Book value with a haircut Uncertain sale value and timing

Step 4: add up and bridge to equity

Sum of the parts (gross asset value)
− net debt of the holding company
− perpetual securities and minority interests (if not already netted)
= equity value
÷ shares outstanding
= SOTP value per share

Step 5: apply (or not) a holding discount

Markets often value groups like Keppel below the sum of their parts, and analysts commonly apply a discount to the gross value. State the discount you use and why. If Keppel's asset-light plan works, the discount should shrink: that is a possible investment argument.

Step 6: cross-check with other methods

  • Price to book (P/B) versus return on equity (ROE): a company earning an ROE above its cost of equity deserves a P/B above 1.
  • Dividend yield: compare Keppel's yield with peers and with the Singapore 10-year bond yield.
  • Keppel's own RNAV per share, where the company publishes it, compared with the share price.

Step 7: sensitivity

Show how the value per share moves with the two inputs that matter most, for example the multiple on asset management earnings and the holding discount.

Step 8: target price and recommendation

Set the 12-month target from the SOTP (after discount), round it sensibly, compare it with the price and apply stated rating bands (for example buy above +15%, sell below −10%, hold in between).

Common mistakes

  • Valuing a listed stake at book value when its market price is available.
  • Counting the same asset twice (for example inside "asset management" and again as a REIT stake).
  • Forgetting perpetual securities: they behave like debt for ordinary shareholders.
  • Using peer multiples on different bases (trailing versus forward).
  • A target price that does not match the recommendation.

9. How to collect these inputs, step by step (for a total beginner)

What SOTP means. A sum-of-the-parts valuation values a group like a basket of separate businesses. You value each part with the method that fits it best, add them up, subtract the group's net debt and other claims, and divide by the number of shares. Analogy: to price a house sold with a car and a boat, you price each one separately rather than applying one rule to the whole lot.

What RNAV means. RNAV (revalued net asset value) is a version of the balance sheet where each asset is marked at its current market or fair value instead of its accounting book value, minus all debts. Divided by the number of shares, it gives an "RNAV per share". The share price is often compared with it ("trading at a 25% discount to RNAV").

Steps: 1. Share price. Open the Yahoo chart link (https://query1.finance.yahoo.com/v8/finance/chart/BN4.SI?range=2y&interval=1d) or the SGX quote page, and note the close for the valuation date (13 Nov 2026 = S$9.82). Cross-check with the SGX API. 2. Share count. Open the latest results announcement (1H 2026, Keppel IR site or Quartr). Find the line "ordinary shares (excluding treasury shares)" under the NAV per share note. Treasury shares are shares the company bought back; they do not count. 3. Market cap = price x share count. 4. Net debt and other claims. In the balance sheet, take the loans plus lease liabilities, minus cash. Also note perpetual securities (debt-like hybrid bonds) and non-controlling interests (the part of subsidiaries owned by others). All of these are subtracted from the SOTP total. 5. Listed stakes. In the results deck, find the "List of REITs/Trust" slide: Keppel's % stake and the market value of the stake. Update it with today's unit price: value today = value then x (price today / price then). 6. Fee business. Take asset management fees and asset management net profit from the deck, and apply a P/E multiple taken from listed asset managers (section 6). 7. Private fund stakes and non-core assets. Use the carrying (book) values given in the deck. Apply a discount if you think a sale would fetch less. 8. Operating businesses (power, data centres, technology solutions). Take segment EBITDA or net profit and apply an EV/EBITDA or P/E multiple from comparable companies such as Sembcorp. 9. Cost of capital. Risk-free rate = Singapore 10-year government bond yield (MAS website). Equity risk premium = Damodaran's website. Beta = how much Keppel's share moves when the market moves: regress two years of weekly Keppel returns on STI returns (done here in the weekly CSV; beta 1.12). Cost of equity = risk-free + beta x ERP. 10. Cross-checks. Compare your SOTP value per share with the broker targets (section 5), with the NAV per share (5.60) and with peer P/E and P/B multiples. Keppel Ltd (SGX: BN4), Industrials / Conglomerates. Preliminary recommendation: HOLD. Price S$9.82 (13 Nov 2026). Preliminary target price S$9.32 (-5%).

  1. The price already assumes the transformation largely works. At 1.75 times book, the market is paying for a sustainable group ROE of about 11%, against 7.4% in FY2025 and 3.6% annualised in 1H2026.

What would make us more positive: faster cash proceeds from the non-core portfolio, an end to rig impairments, or fee growth that keeps pace with the S$200bn FUM target. Our value would then move towards the consensus target of S$12.71.

The market

Term What it means
Share price What one share costs on the stock exchange today. Keppel trades on the Singapore Exchange (SGX) in Singapore dollars (SGD).
Ticker The short code of a share: BN4 for Keppel on SGX.
Market capitalisation (market cap) Share price × number of shares. What the market says the whole company's equity is worth.
Enterprise value (EV) Market cap + debt − cash (+ minority interests and leases). The value of the business itself, whoever finances it. A company with lots of cash has an EV below its market cap.
Target price Our estimate of the share price in 12 months.
Upside / downside (Target price − current price) / current price.
Recommendation Buy, hold or sell. A common convention: buy if upside is above about 15%, sell if downside is beyond about −10%, hold in between. We fix our own bands and state them.
Consensus The average forecast of the analysts who follow the stock.
Broker A bank or firm that publishes research (DBS, UBS, Jefferies...). Their reports are "broker views", never facts.

The company's accounts

Term What it means
Revenue Total sales in the period.
Gross profit, gross margin Revenue minus the direct cost of making the products; margin = gross profit / revenue.
Operating profit (EBIT) Profit after all operating costs (staff, R&D, rent...), before interest and tax.
EBITDA EBIT + depreciation and amortisation. A rough measure of operating cash generation.
Net profit What is left for shareholders after interest and tax.
EPS Earnings per share = net profit / number of shares.
D&A (depreciation and amortisation) The yearly cost of wearing out machines and intangible assets, spread over their life.
Capex Capital expenditure: money spent on equipment and buildings.
Free cash flow (FCF) Cash from operations − capex. Cash the company could hand back to investors.
Working capital Inventory + receivables − payables: money tied up in running the business.
Inventory days Inventory / cost of sales × 365. How long products sit in the warehouse.
Receivable days Trade receivables / revenue × 365. How long customers take to pay.
ROE Return on equity = net profit / shareholders' equity.
ROIC Return on invested capital = after-tax operating profit / (equity + net debt). Compared with WACC, it tells you if growth creates value.
Net cash Cash − borrowings. Positive = more cash than debt.
1H2026, FY2025 First half of 2026 (Jan-Jun); full financial year 2025 (Keppel's year ends 31 Dec).

Valuation

Term What it means
DCF (discounted cash flow) Value = sum of future free cash flows, each divided by (1 + WACC) to the power of its year, plus a terminal value. Answers: what is the business worth if these forecasts come true?
FCFF Free cash flow to the firm = EBIT × (1 − tax) + D&A − capex − increase in working capital.
WACC Weighted average cost of capital: the return investors require, mixing the cost of equity and the cost of debt by their weights.
Cost of equity (CAPM) Risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium.
Risk-free rate Yield on a safe government bond, here the 10-year Singapore Government Security.
Equity risk premium (ERP) The extra return investors want for holding shares instead of safe bonds (about 4-6% a year in most estimates).
Beta How much the share moves when the market moves. Beta 1.5 = moves 1.5% for each 1% of the market.
Terminal value The value of all cash flows after the last forecast year, usually FCF × (1 + g) / (WACC − g).
g (terminal growth) Long-run growth rate assumed forever after the forecast, usually 2-3%.
Multiples / comparables (comps) Valuing Keppel by comparing it with similar listed companies: P/E, EV/EBITDA, EV/Sales.
P/E Price / earnings per share. "a share trades at 20x earnings" means investors pay 20 dollars per dollar of yearly profit.
Forward vs trailing Forward multiples use next year's forecast earnings; trailing ones use the last 12 months. Never mix the two.
Sensitivity table A grid showing how the target price changes when two inputs change (e.g. WACC and g).
Football field A chart of value ranges from each method, side by side, with the current price drawn across.

Keppel and asset management

Term What it means
Asset manager A firm that invests other people's money (pension funds, insurers, sovereign funds) and charges fees for it. Keppel now presents itself as a global asset manager and operator.
Operator A firm that also runs the assets day to day (power plants, data centres, buildings), which brings operating income on top of fees.
FUM (funds under management) The total value of the money and assets a manager runs for clients. Fees are usually a percentage of FUM.
Management fee A yearly fee, typically a small percentage of FUM or of committed capital. Stable and recurring.
Performance fee / carried interest A share of the profits a fund makes above a target return. Large but irregular.
Recurring income Income that comes back every year (fees, rents, operating income), as opposed to one-off gains from selling assets.
Asset monetisation Selling assets the group owns on its balance sheet to release cash (Keppel's programme since 2022).
Asset-light A model where the group owns fewer assets itself and earns more from fees on assets owned by its funds.
REIT / business trust A listed vehicle that owns income-producing property or infrastructure and pays out most of its income. Keppel sponsors several (Keppel DC REIT, Keppel REIT, Keppel Infrastructure Trust...).
Sponsor The group that created a REIT or trust, keeps a stake in it and manages it for a fee.
Legacy offshore and marine assets Rigs and vessels left over from Keppel's former shipbuilding business, kept outside the core business after the 2023 combination with Sembcorp Marine (now Seatrium).
Conglomerate discount When the market values a group with many businesses below the sum of what each business would be worth on its own.
Sum of the parts (SOTP) Valuing each business separately with the method that fits it, adding the values, then subtracting net debt.
RNAV (revalued net asset value) The value of the group's assets at market or fair value minus its liabilities, per share. Keppel publishes its own figure.
Net gearing Net debt / total equity. Shows how much the group borrows relative to its own funds.
Temasek Singapore's state investment company, Keppel's largest shareholder.

ESG

Term What it means
ESG Environmental, social and governance: the non-financial risks and strengths of a company.
Scope 1 / Scope 2 emissions Scope 1 = greenhouse gases the company emits itself; Scope 2 = emissions from the electricity it buys.

Materiality An ESG issue is material if it can change the company's value (for Keppel: carbon intensity of its power assets, the energy transition, governance and past compliance issues).
