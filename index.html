Everything below comes from the Official Rules of the 2027 CFA Institute Research Challenge (22 pages, "2026-2027 season") and the CFA Society Singapore kick-off slides of 8 Sep 2026. Page numbers are given so anyone can check.

What we are writing

An equity research report: the kind of document a bank analyst writes to tell investors whether to buy, hold or sell a share, and at what price it should trade in 12 months (the target price). We write it "from the perspective of an independent research analyst", using public information only (Rules p. 9, 16).

Format of the written report

  • At most 10 A4 pages, plus an appendix of at most 10 A4 pages. The official CFA front cover does not count, and we may only fill in its highlighted fields (Rules p. 16).
  • The first page must show: company name, exchange, ticker, sector, recommendation (buy/hold/sell), current price with its date, and target price with the % up or down (Rules p. 16).
  • Seven sections are required: Business description; Industry overview and competitive positioning; Investment summary; Valuation; Financial analysis; Investment risks; Environmental, social and governance (Rules p. 16).
  • Every fact, chart and table is cited in one system. The team uses Harvard style: (Keppel Ltd, 2026, p. 12) in the text and a reference list at the end (Rules p. 4, 16).

Help we are allowed, and the limits

  • Faculty advisor (Prof. Rick Marchese): at most 10 hours before the report is submitted. Industry mentor (Samir Mehta, CFA): at most 6 hours. Going over means disqualification of the team (Rules p. 10-11). Every meeting goes in the help-hours log.
  • They can guide and give feedback, but cannot write content or do analysis for us (Rules p. 10).
  • No other finance professional may help, including mock judging panels (Rules p. 7, 9, 11).
  • Broker reports (UBS, Jefferies, DBS...) can be read, but anything we use from them must be cited as a broker view. Copying them is plagiarism and means disqualification (Rules p. 4-5, 8).
  • Each member must disclose any shares held in Keppel (Rules p. 4, cover p. 18).

Artificial intelligence

AI tools are allowed "in a responsible, transparent, and ethically sound manner", but material use must be disclosed and documented, and presenting AI-generated content as wholly original human work is prohibited (Rules p. 8). Judges are likely to ask about it (Rules p. 17), so each of us should be able to say: which tool, for what, how we checked the output, and what we decided ourselves. This site keeps that log on the page "AI use log".

  2. Financial analysis: five years of history in a table, the main ratios computed and explained.
  3. Forecast: a first version for 2026-2028 with the key assumptions stated, even if rough.
  4. Valuation: a first DCF and a peer comparison, giving a provisional value range, clearly labelled "preliminary".
  5. Risks and ESG: the list of risks with a short explanation each; the main ESG facts.

Part C: Forecast and investment risks

Owner: Juliette; owns the Excel model. Reviewer: Part B. Report sections: Financial analysis, forecast (shared 20 points with Part B) and Investment risks (15 points).

What the judges want

Financial analysis is worth 20 points and is shared between the past (Part B) and the forecast (this part). Investment risks (15 points, Rules p. 19) are covered further down this page. For the forecast, judges look for a model driven by a few clear assumptions that link back to the business story, compared with management targets and with what other analysts expect, and for honest scenarios rather than one single "right" number.

How to build the forecast for Keppel, step by step

Step 1: forecast profit by segment, not revenue alone

Keppel's revenue mixes very different businesses, so the useful forecast is net profit by segment (Infrastructure, Real Estate, Connectivity, plus corporate costs), and inside it the asset management fees. For each part ask what drives it:

  • asset management fees follow funds under management (FUM) and the fee rate;
  • infrastructure profit follows power prices, contracted capacity and new projects;
  • real estate profit follows property sales and rents;
  • connectivity follows data-centre capacity and occupancy;
  • one-off gains come from asset sales (the asset monetisation programme) and should be kept separate.

Write each assumption as one sentence with a source ("Keppel had S$... of FUM at 30 Jun 2026 (Keppel Ltd, 2026b, p. x)").

Step 2: separate recurring income from one-offs

Judges reward teams that show how much of the profit comes back every year. Forecast recurring income and one-off gains on separate lines.

Step 3: check against three anchors

  1. Management targets: Vision 2030, FUM targets, asset monetisation targets, dividend policy.
  2. Consensus: what analysts expect on average (label it as a broker view).
  3. History: the segment profits of the last five years (Part B).

If our numbers are far from management or consensus, explain why in one sentence. That gap is often the heart of the investment thesis.

Step 4: balance sheet and cash

Forecast net debt from operating cash flow plus asset sale proceeds, minus investments and dividends. Net gearing (net debt / equity) should stay in the range management has given.

Step 5: three scenarios

Bull, base and bear, each with one coherent story (for example bear = fundraising slows and asset sales are delayed). Give each a rough probability. The valuation runs on each scenario.

Step 6: hand the numbers to Part D

One table per year, 2026 to 2028: FUM, fee income, segment net profit, recurring income, one-offs, net debt, dividends per share. Part D reads them and does not change them.

Part D: Valuation and target price

Owner: Baptiste. Reviewer: Part A. Report section: Valuation (20 points).

What the judges want

Valuation is worth 20 of the 100 report points (Rules p. 19). CFA Institute's guidance says a strong valuation uses more than one method, explains every key input, and ends with a target price that follows from the numbers (CFA Institute, 2020, p. 3). For a group like Keppel, judges expect a sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) valuation, because one single multiple cannot capture a business that mixes fee income, operating assets, property and listed stakes. They check three things: is each part valued with a method that fits it, are the inputs sourced, and is the gap to the share price explained (the "conglomerate discount").

How to do this part, step by step

Step 1: fix the price and the share count

Take the closing price on one date and use it everywhere (for the preliminary report: Friday 13 Nov 2026). Shares outstanding come from the latest company filing, net of treasury shares. Price × shares = market capitalisation.

Step 2: list the parts

Write down every block of value Keppel owns, in the way the company reports it. Typically:

  1. the asset management business (fees earned on funds under management);
  2. stakes in listed REITs and trusts it sponsors (Keppel DC REIT, Keppel REIT, Keppel Infrastructure Trust...);
  3. operating infrastructure businesses (power, renewables, environmental infrastructure);
  4. real estate it owns directly (land bank, development projects);
  5. connectivity (data centres, subsea cables, and any telecom stake);
  6. non-core and legacy assets (for example the legacy offshore and marine assets);
  7. net debt, perpetual securities and minority interests, which are subtracted at the end.

Step 3: value each part with the method that fits

Part Usual method Why
Asset management Multiple of fee-related earnings (P/E of listed asset managers) Fees are recurring; peers such as Brookfield or CapitaLand Investment give the multiple
Listed REIT and trust stakes Market value: number of units × unit price on the same date The market already prices them
Operating infrastructure DCF or EV/EBITDA of utility and infrastructure peers Long-lived, cash-generating assets
Real estate held directly Book value or revalued value (RNAV), possibly with a discount Property values are disclosed in the accounts
Connectivity, data centres EV/EBITDA of data-centre peers, or book value Growth assets; few earnings yet
Non-core and legacy Book value with a haircut Uncertain sale value and timing

Step 4: add up and bridge to equity

Sum of the parts (gross asset value)
− net debt of the holding company
− perpetual securities and minority interests (if not already netted)
= equity value
÷ shares outstanding
= SOTP value per share

Step 5: apply (or not) a holding discount

Markets often value groups like Keppel below the sum of their parts, and analysts commonly apply a discount to the gross value. State the discount you use and why. If Keppel's asset-light plan works, the discount should shrink: that is a possible investment argument.

Step 6: cross-check with other methods

  • Price to book (P/B) versus return on equity (ROE): a company earning an ROE above its cost of equity deserves a P/B above 1.
  • Dividend yield: compare Keppel's yield with peers and with the Singapore 10-year bond yield.
  • Keppel's own RNAV per share, where the company publishes it, compared with the share price.

Step 7: sensitivity

Show how the value per share moves with the two inputs that matter most, for example the multiple on asset management earnings and the holding discount.

Step 8: target price and recommendation

Set the 12-month target from the SOTP (after discount), round it sensibly, compare it with the price and apply stated rating bands (for example buy above +15%, sell below −10%, hold in between).

Common mistakes

  • Valuing a listed stake at book value when its market price is available.
  • Counting the same asset twice (for example inside "asset management" and again as a REIT stake).
  • Forgetting perpetual securities: they behave like debt for ordinary shareholders.
  • Using peer multiples on different bases (trailing versus forward).
  • A target price that does not match the recommendation.

Glossary: every term used on this site

Plain definitions, in the order you are likely to meet them. When a term has a formula, the formula is given.

The market

Term What it means
Share price What one share costs on the stock exchange today. Keppel trades on the Singapore Exchange (SGX) in Singapore dollars (SGD).
Ticker The short code of a share: BN4 for Keppel on SGX.
Market capitalisation (market cap) Share price × number of shares. What the market says the whole company's equity is worth.
Enterprise value (EV) Market cap + debt − cash (+ minority interests and leases). The value of the business itself, whoever finances it. A company with lots of cash has an EV below its market cap.
Target price Our estimate of the share price in 12 months.
Upside / downside (Target price − current price) / current price.
Recommendation Buy, hold or sell. A common convention: buy if upside is above about 15%, sell if downside is beyond about −10%, hold in between. We fix our own bands and state them.
Consensus The average forecast of the analysts who follow the stock.
Broker A bank or firm that publishes research (DBS, UBS, Jefferies...). Their reports are "broker views", never facts.

The company's accounts

Term What it means
Revenue Total sales in the period.
Gross profit, gross margin Revenue minus the direct cost of making the products; margin = gross profit / revenue.
Operating profit (EBIT) Profit after all operating costs (staff, R&D, rent...), before interest and tax.
EBITDA EBIT + depreciation and amortisation. A rough measure of operating cash generation.
Net profit What is left for shareholders after interest and tax.
EPS Earnings per share = net profit / number of shares.
D&A (depreciation and amortisation) The yearly cost of wearing out machines and intangible assets, spread over their life.
Capex Capital expenditure: money spent on equipment and buildings.
Free cash flow (FCF) Cash from operations − capex. Cash the company could hand back to investors.
Working capital Inventory + receivables − payables: money tied up in running the business.
Inventory days Inventory / cost of sales × 365. How long products sit in the warehouse.
Receivable days Trade receivables / revenue × 365. How long customers take to pay.
ROE Return on equity = net profit / shareholders' equity.
ROIC Return on invested capital = after-tax operating profit / (equity + net debt). Compared with WACC, it tells you if growth creates value.
Net cash Cash − borrowings. Positive = more cash than debt.
1H2026, FY2025 First half of 2026 (Jan-Jun); full financial year 2025 (Keppel's year ends 31 Dec).

Valuation

Term What it means
DCF (discounted cash flow) Value = sum of future free cash flows, each divided by (1 + WACC) to the power of its year, plus a terminal value. Answers: what is the business worth if these forecasts come true?
FCFF Free cash flow to the firm = EBIT × (1 − tax) + D&A − capex − increase in working capital.
WACC Weighted average cost of capital: the return investors require, mixing the cost of equity and the cost of debt by their weights.
Cost of equity (CAPM) Risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium.
Risk-free rate Yield on a safe government bond, here the 10-year Singapore Government Security.
Equity risk premium (ERP) The extra return investors want for holding shares instead of safe bonds (about 4-6% a year in most estimates).
Beta How much the share moves when the market moves. Beta 1.5 = moves 1.5% for each 1% of the market.
Terminal value The value of all cash flows after the last forecast year, usually FCF × (1 + g) / (WACC − g).
g (terminal growth) Long-run growth rate assumed forever after the forecast, usually 2-3%.
Multiples / comparables (comps) Valuing Keppel by comparing it with similar listed companies: P/E, EV/EBITDA, EV/Sales.
P/E Price / earnings per share. "a share trades at 20x earnings" means investors pay 20 dollars per dollar of yearly profit.
Forward vs trailing Forward multiples use next year's forecast earnings; trailing ones use the last 12 months. Never mix the two.
Sensitivity table A grid showing how the target price changes when two inputs change (e.g. WACC and g).
Football field A chart of value ranges from each method, side by side, with the current price drawn across.

Keppel and asset management

Term What it means
Asset manager A firm that invests other people's money (pension funds, insurers, sovereign funds) and charges fees for it. Keppel now presents itself as a global asset manager and operator.
Operator A firm that also runs the assets day to day (power plants, data centres, buildings), which brings operating income on top of fees.
FUM (funds under management) The total value of the money and assets a manager runs for clients. Fees are usually a percentage of FUM.
Management fee A yearly fee, typically a small percentage of FUM or of committed capital. Stable and recurring.
Performance fee / carried interest A share of the profits a fund makes above a target return. Large but irregular.
Recurring income Income that comes back every year (fees, rents, operating income), as opposed to one-off gains from selling assets.
Asset monetisation Selling assets the group owns on its balance sheet to release cash (Keppel's programme since 2022).
Asset-light A model where the group owns fewer assets itself and earns more from fees on assets owned by its funds.
REIT / business trust A listed vehicle that owns income-producing property or infrastructure and pays out most of its income. Keppel sponsors several (Keppel DC REIT, Keppel REIT, Keppel Infrastructure Trust...).
Sponsor The group that created a REIT or trust, keeps a stake in it and manages it for a fee.
Legacy offshore and marine assets Rigs and vessels left over from Keppel's former shipbuilding business, kept outside the core business after the 2023 combination with Sembcorp Marine (now Seatrium).
Conglomerate discount When the market values a group with many businesses below the sum of what each business would be worth on its own.
Sum of the parts (SOTP) Valuing each business separately with the method that fits it, adding the values, then subtracting net debt.
RNAV (revalued net asset value) The value of the group's assets at market or fair value minus its liabilities, per share. Keppel publishes its own figure.
Net gearing Net debt / total equity. Shows how much the group borrows relative to its own funds.
Temasek Singapore's state investment company, Keppel's largest shareholder.

ESG

Term What it means
ESG Environmental, social and governance: the non-financial risks and strengths of a company.
Scope 1 / Scope 2 emissions Scope 1 = greenhouse gases the company emits itself; Scope 2 = emissions from the electricity it buys.
Board independence Share of directors with no link to management or major shareholders.
Materiality An ESG issue is material if it can change the company's value (for Keppel: carbon intensity of its power assets, the energy transition, governance and past compliance issues).
